  [2025-07-08] Avery Pennarun
  https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7348372272853766146
  ............................................................
  Everyone thinks “simple” is easy. In reality, it’s one of the hardest
  things to do — especially over time.

  Take Tailscale. We had one platform when we launched in 2019. We still
  have one in 2025. And, if we do our jobs right, we’ll still have one in
  2030.

  That’s not because we’ve been sitting still. Last year I quote-skeeted
  someone on Bluesky who said they could build Tailscale in a weekend. My
  reply: “Sure. And we’ve been debugging it ever since.”
  https://lnkd.in/gAEt9NMf

  It was a joke, but it’s also kind of the whole point. It’s easy to ship
  something once. It’s hard to keep refining it, improving it, simplifying
  it — especially when everything around you pushes toward adding more.

  Most companies in our space start with one product. Then they add a
  second. Then a third. Eventually they have a “suite.” That’s a valid
  strategy, and sometimes it works well. But somewhere along the way, the
  clarity of the original idea gets blurry.

  For me, and for us at Tailscale, simplicity isn’t a constraint — it’s
  the mission. Making networks just work, without configuration files or
  VPN gateways or “quickstart” guides longer than your arm.

  I wrote a whole blog post last year about this vision for a new kind of
  Internet:
  https://lnkd.in/gdzMR-AC

  It’s not about nostalgia (but yes, it was a reference to the show
  Silicon Valley that kind of took on a life of its own!) It’s about how
  things should work. Direct, secure, peer-to-peer. The kind of simplicity
  that’s invisible because it’s doing its job.

  And yes, we’re a business. If we keep doing this well, we’ll make money.
  But the focus — on simplicity, usability, and delight — is what keeps me
  going every day.

  So I’m curious:
  What’s your version of simplicity?
  What’s the thing you’ve resisted overbuilding, sometimes at great cost,
  because it mattered more to keep it clear?

  I’d love to hear your stories.

  [2025-07-21] Avery Pennarun
  https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7353066113229836288
  ............................................................
  You’ve probably seen the story: a UK logistics company, Knights of Old,
  was taken offline by a single bad password. https://lnkd.in/gi47iuNA

  First — let's have some empathy. Nobody wants to be that company. And
  yet, even in 2025, this keeps happening. We’ve spent years telling
  people to use strong, unique passwords. But here’s the uncomfortable
  truth: even if you do everything right with passwords, it still might
  not be enough.

  That’s because a password (or a login link, or a YubiKey, or Face ID...)
  only handles authentication. It answers the question: Are you who you
  say you are?

  But the more important question is: Now that you're in, what are you
  allowed to do? That’s authorization. And most networks don’t separate
  those two concerns very well.

  At Tailscale, we don’t manage your credentials — we delegate
  authentication to providers like Google, Microsoft, Okta, Apple, etc.
  But that’s just step one.

  Step two is defining policy: who can access what, from where, when, and
  how. We use Grants and ACLs (access control lists) and service-level
  rules to make sure that even if a password leaks, the attacker doesn't
  get the whole network. They get only what the legitimate user was
  authorized to access — which might be a dev sandbox and not, say,
  production systems.

  Think of it like this: authentication gives you the front door key.
  Authorization decides whether that key opens just the guest bathroom or
  the vault in the basement.

  Layer on an MDM service like Jamf or Kandji, , and suddenly even access
  to the guest bathroom requires you to be wearing long pants instead of
  shorts. Because security is about posture (and pants), not just
  presence. ;)

  Passwords still matter. But policies matter more. If you haven’t updated
  your authorization model in a while, don’t wait for a ransomware
  headline to remind you.

  What’s your version of “long pants required” when it comes to network
  access? 👖

  I read a post in Wired this week about how some corners of the tech
  industry are embracing the controversial 996 work schedule — 9am to 9pm,
  six days a week. (Link in comments.)

  It reminded me of something we’ve all seen before.

  Back in the early 2000s, the story was outsourcing.
  “Developers overseas will replace your whole team for a tenth the cost.”
  At the same time:
  “Can your team move faster? We’re falling behind. Maybe they just don’t
  want it enough.”

  Now, we’ve replaced “outsourcing” with “AI.”
  “LLMs will write the code, do the tests, fix the bugs.”
  Also:
  “By the way, we need you to commit to a 996 schedule. 72 hours a week.
  You’re either all-in, or you’re out.”

  We’re told that human work is replaceable — but also, we need to give
  more of ourselves. Just in case.

  I don’t believe in that framing. The contradiction is exhausting.

  Good work takes time. It takes energy and space.
  Teams that are well-rested build better systems. They make better
  decisions. They support each other through the messy parts — and they
  don’t burn out after sprint three.

  You can love your work. A lot of us do. But your work can't love you
  back. It won't protect your weekends. It won't tell you to close the
  laptop and go for a walk.

  That’s our job. As leaders, as teammates.

  The goal isn’t just to build great products. It’s to build them in a way
  that’s sustainable, respectful, and — ideally — kind.

  And if that sounds slow? It’s probably faster in the long run.

  Last week WorkOS hosted MCP Night 2.0, and there was one question on
  seemingly everyone’s mind…

  How do we make MCP ready for the enterprise?

  From the panel with Anthropic & OpenAI, to nearly everyone I spoke with
  at the afterparty, (yes, you read that right, afterparty 🎉) enterprise
  adoption was top of mind.

  While it’s obvious that improved security and observability are the
  answer, it goes well beyond attaching some OAuth and logging to a public
  MCP server.

  Using LLMs with MCPs, either as yourself or agenticly, while incredibly
  helpful, opens you and your data up to a multitude of attack vectors,
  including:
  - Data Exfiltration via Prompt Injection
  - Malicious MCP / Tool Use
  - Poor Server Configuration / Implementation
  - Sandbox Escapes
  - Credential Theft or Reuse
  - Supply Chain Compromises
  - Protocol Issues / Attacks

  While this might not scare off someone using an LLM with web browsing to
  research vacations destinations, it definitely gives IT & Security a
  real reason to worry.

  At Tailscale we’re looking at new ways to leverage our combination of
  secure network connectivity and identity to help secure MCPs in the
  enterprise.

  However, it’s clear that something this far reaching and important is
  most certainly going to be a group effort.

  So, if you’re also tackling the MCP security problem, DM me and let’s
  chat!

  #mcp #llms

  Zero Trust was supposed to kill the VPN.

  It didn’t. In fact, the business VPN market is booming — projected to
  nearly double in size over the next decade.

  Why? Because real-world networking is messy. Because migrating to Zero
  Trust is hard. Because security that's technically right often gets in
  the way of people just trying to do their jobs. I’ve written five
  different VPNs over 25 years. Not because I like VPNs (I really don’t)
  but because the alternatives keep turning out worse.

  In my latest piece for TechRadar, I look at why VPNs are still
  everywhere, and what it’ll actually take to move forward. Not through
  buzzwords, but through systems that make security usable. That make
  sense.

  Tailscale wasn’t built to reinvent VPNs for the fun of it. We’re here
  because people still need secure access that works, without friction,
  without tradeoffs, without trusting the whole network.

  If we do this right, maybe next time we won’t have to rebuild the same
  tunnel, again. I'm certainly hoping fifth time's a charm!

  I’ve been thinking a lot about the “edge” lately. Not the buzzwordy one
  in slide decks, but the real edge: the scruffy little embedded devices
  running the world while nobody’s looking.

  Forbes just published a piece I wrote (link in comments) about exactly
  that: why the most critical infrastructure in 2025 isn’t running in the
  cloud, or on a desktop OS, or even patched on any predictable schedule,
  it’s running quietly, out of sight, on tiny computers that rarely get
  updated and never get the attention they deserve.

  What makes this fun is seeing how companies are actually solving these
  problems today.

  Two great examples:
  Dexory is out there running entire fleets of autonomous warehouse
  robots, where “just reboot it” is not a plan and the network has to work
  even when everything around it is noisy, dusty, or generally behaving
  like a warehouse does on its busiest day.

  Meanwhile, DEEL Media is managing thousands of digital displays
  scattered across retail environments where nobody has time, or the
  desire, to SSH into anything ever again. Both of them are great examples
  of what we keep seeing at the edge: real companies solving real problems
  on devices that quietly run the world.

  And they’re not alone. Every week we hear from more teams, big
  enterprises, small startups, and a surprising number of folks who deploy
  Raspberry Pis into places Raspberry Pis were never meant to go (Andrew
  McCalip, I'm looking at you!) all using Tailscale to make edge
  connectivity simple, secure, and boring in the best possible way.

  The pattern is the same everywhere: you can’t rely on perfect firmware,
  perfect networks, or perfect device management. So you design systems
  that stay safe even when nothing else is perfect.

  Because the truth is simple: the world doesn’t run on macOS or Windows
  on high-end laptops. It runs on Linux on robots, kiosks, factory
  sensors, and a million tiny embedded devices nobody ever remembers to
  update.

  If we secure that world properly, everything else gets a lot better too.

  What’s the strangest, scrappiest, or “I can’t believe this actually
  works” place you’ve seen Tailscale running?

  Fraudulent Candidates Are Everywhere—Here’s What We’re Doing About It

  Over the last few months, we’ve seen a huge rise in fraudulent
  applicants here at Tailscale. In some cases, as many as half of
  applicants on a job are not who they say they are. Yes, really.

  The good news: we’ve gotten very good at spotting it, and I wanted to
  share what I have learned.

  🎯 Who’s a Target?
  -remote-first tech companies
  -companies with fully remote interview processes

  ❓ Why? Fraudsters are hoping they can:
  -impersonate someone else
  -use deepfake video or audio
  -bypass less-rigorous screening steps
  -eventually steal data or paychecks

  Basically, anywhere the hiring process happens behind a laptop, fraud is
  rising.

  🛑 The Most Telltale Signs:
  -no profile photo (or a cartoon avatar- as much as we love them!) on
  LinkedIn
  -no connections or a brand-new LinkedIn account
  -application language suspiciously close to the exact verbiage in your
  job description
  -different name on the resume vs email vs LinkedIn
  (not just a nickname… literally different identities)
  -repeated applications even after being rejected
  -listing n/a in job posting questions to bypass or writing nonsensical
  answers

  ⚠️ Important:
  One flag alone does not mean fraud—but multiple flags together should
  absolutely make you pause and verify.

  Some Tips
  👉 Message them on LinkedIn before the interview
  If the LinkedIn looks legit but something else feels off, send a message
  and ask them to confirm the interview time.
  If it’s a fraudulent applicant, 1 of 2 things might happen:
  1) the real person responds (“um…who??”) 2) the scammer disappears
  Either way, you get clarity without wasting time.
  👉 Email before the interview. You can literally say: “We’ve been seeing
  a lot of fraudulent applications—would you mind confirming X?” Most
  legitimate candidates won’t mind at all.
  👉 For Engineers, ask for a GitHub link in your application questions &
  have them to add you to a private repo (takes 30 seconds).
  👉 Use verification tools. We use tofu, and it’s been excellent. It can
  tell you:
  -if their email address or LinkedIn was created yesterday
  -whether the email matches the LinkedIn signup
  -if the phone number is tied to prior scams
  -whether the same resume shows up under multiple names at your company
  or the countless others in their network

  It’s worth the investment—especially if you’re remote and high-volume.

  💡 Remember:
  Behind every scam attempt, there’s sometimes a real person whose
  identity is being abused. If you confirm something is fraudulent, be
  kind and send a quick InMail to the person. Most have no idea someone
  applied on their behalf.

  TL;DR
  Fraudulent applicants are here, they’re getting more sophisticated, and
  we’re not tolerating it. At Tailscale, we’re actively verifying
  identity, tightening processes, and investing in tools. If you’re a
  hiring manager or recruiter dealing with the same, I hope this helps.
  And if you’re a scammer thinking of applying here… please don’t. We’re
  onto you. 😉

  LEGO quietly shipped a self-organizing network made out of plastic
  bricks.

  No hub. No app. No screen. Their new Smart Bricks (link in comments)
  discover each other over Bluetooth, share state, and coordinate
  behavior. LEGO calls it BrickNet, which is a charming name for “there’s
  a distributed system in your kid’s Millennium Falcon now.”

  As a networking person, I love this.

  It’s small, local, and purpose-built. The network exists only to make
  the toy work, then mostly disappears. No cloud account, no dashboard, no
  SaaS subscription for the left thruster. That’s actually a very sane
  design choice.

  It’s also where the interesting questions start.

  Bluetooth instead of Wi-Fi means a different trust model: proximity as
  authentication, pairing semantics nobody ever sees, keys that may
  outlive the play session they were meant for. “No setup” doesn’t mean no
  security decisions, it just means they were made for you, ahead of time.

  None of this is a complaint. Quite the opposite. This is networking at a
  human scale: ephemeral, local, and mostly invisible. Honestly, it’s
  probably more robust than half the IoT gadgets people install in their
  homes.

  Still, toys are real networks now, running real protocols, in the same
  rooms as everything else we care about. At some point two fleets of star
  destroyers meet, or someone figures out how to talk to the bricks in an
  unexpected way, and the boundary between “play” and “infrastructure”
  gets fuzzy.

  If my kids' LEGO set starts advertising routes, I’m definitely asking
  where the ACLs live.

  A lot of security work starts as checkbox security.

  This year, one of the most effective checkboxes seems to be cyber
  insurance. Not because insurers are security experts, but because they
  keep asking questions that are awkwardly hard to answer if your
  privileged access story is “uhhh… Dave has the admin password.”

  Sometimes that deserves skepticism. But PAM is one of the rare
  checkboxes that points at a real problem.

  A framing that seems to help is this: “I don’t actually want a user on
  the network. I want them to reach this service.” That mental shift
  removes a surprising number of headaches.

  Privileged access is where accidents become incidents. Long-lived admin
  credentials, shared secrets, broad access “just in case.” Those
  shortcuts feel efficient right up until they aren’t.

  What I worry about, especially for teams tackling PAM seriously for the
  first time, is that the default solution is often “buy a vault.” Put the
  powerful passwords somewhere safer, rotate them more often, add
  approvals, record sessions. Better than nothing, but also a lot of
  machinery to manage a thing that maybe shouldn’t exist in the first
  place.

  The simpler model is: stop handing out standing privilege. Use identity.
  Use policy. Make access just-in-time, tightly scoped, and auditable.
  Then take that access away automatically.

  This is where the network layer helps more than people expect. If access
  is tied to identity and enforced by ACLs, you do not need humans
  carrying secrets around at all. You decide who can talk to what, under
  which conditions, and you log it.

  At Tailscale, people often arrive thinking about connectivity. They
  leave thinking about access. And once you start from identity plus
  policy, a bunch of “traditional PAM” complexity just stops being
  necessary.

  Insurance and audits might be what gets people to look at PAM. The best

  AI agents are getting more powerful.

  They’re also getting a little unhinged 😅

  One example: OpenClaw.

  It can access your system, run commands, call APIs, and make
  unpredicatable decisions.

  Fun? Yes.
  Safe? Not really.

  We’re not going to pretend OpenClaw is “safe.”

  But we can reduce the blind spots that make it riskier:

  -where are they spending tokens?

  Yesterday Kevin Kotecki and I had the chance to spend some time with
  Jensen Huang and the team at NVIDIA.

  We’re grateful for the opportunity to share more of what we’ve been
  building at Tailscale—and where we think connectivity needs to go next.

  A lot of the conversation centered around a shift that’s already
  underway: networks are no longer just connecting servers. They’re
  connecting AI systems, robotics, and hardware that was never designed to
  work together, from cutting-edge GPUs to older devices still doing
  critical work.

  That creates a surprisingly hard problem.

  How do you make all of that interoperable without rebuilding everything
  from scratch? How do you give AI systems and physical devices secure,
  identity-based access to each other, across environments, without adding
  layers of complexity that break the moment something changes?

  Our view is that the network has to become simpler, not more
  complicated. More like a shared fabric that different systems can plug
  into, regardless of where they run or when they were built.

  A lot of what we’re doing at Tailscale is aimed at exactly that:
  connecting AI infrastructure, bridging old and new hardware, and making
  it all work as one coherent system.

  It was energizing to compare notes with a team thinking deeply about
  these problems from a completely different layer of the stack.

  Looking forward to continuing the conversation.
